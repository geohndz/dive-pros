Dive Pros — Website Grammar & Design Rules
01 — Write like a diver, not a marketing department
Avoid:
Our award-winning training program provides an exceptional educational experience for divers of all levels.
Prefer:
Learn to dive in our 54,000-gallon heated indoor pool, then take your skills into the Gulf.
Rule:
Specific > impressive.
If a sentence could describe a gym, hotel, agency, or SaaS company, rewrite it.
________________


02 — Kill generic superlatives
Be careful with:
* premier
* world-class
* best
* finest
* exceptional
* amazing
* unparalleled
* unmatched
* trusted
* award-winning
* professional
* state-of-the-art
* comprehensive
* premier destination
The original site uses several of these heavily, including “#1,” “finest,” “award winning,” and “world-class.” (Florida Dive Pros)
Instead, prove the claim.
❌ World-class facilities
✅ 54,000-gallon heated indoor training pool
❌ The best equipment around
✅ Authorized SCUBAPRO and Aqua Lung/Apeks dealer
❌ Professional service
✅ Factory-trained technicians servicing most major SCUBA equipment brands
That's way more persuasive anyway. (Florida Dive Pros)
________________


03 — One idea per sentence
The old site frequently stacks multiple ideas together.
For example, the homepage essentially says:
training + retail + snorkeling + equipment + niches + one-stop shop
all in one paragraph. (Florida Dive Pros)
Instead:
Learn to dive.
Get your gear.
Service your equipment.
Explore Pensacola.
Then explain each one.
Rule:
One sentence = one idea.
________________


04 — Prefer concrete nouns over adjectives
This is a really good anti-AI writing rule.
❌ “Our amazing instructors provide exceptional training.”
✅ “Our instructors teach in a 54,000-gallon heated indoor pool.”
❌ “We offer an incredible selection of equipment.”
✅ “Shop SCUBAPRO, Aqua Lung, Apeks, Shearwater, SeaLife, and more.”
The second versions show expertise instead of announcing it.
________________


05 — Use numbers aggressively
Dive Pros has an enormous advantage:
You have numbers.
Use them.
* Since 1988
* 54,000 gallons
* 12 feet deep
* 2 classrooms
* 2,000+ sq. ft. showroom
* 911 ft. USS Oriskany
* 20+ miles offshore
* $X certification
* X dives
* X days
The original site already contains tons of these facts, but they're buried inside paragraphs. (Florida Dive Pros)
Rule:
If there's a meaningful number, don't hide it in a paragraph.
Make it a visual element.
________________


06 — Don't say “Since 1988” five times
This is important for the redesign.
“Since 1988” is a brand asset, not filler copy.
Use it prominently once.
Then let the rest of the site demonstrate the history through:
* staff
* photos
* testimonials
* facilities
* certifications
* local knowledge
* dive sites
Rule:
A fact becomes weaker when you repeat it.
________________


07 — Don't make every headline a sentence
This is one of the things that can make the site feel AI-generated.
You don't need:
Discover the Gulf Coast's Best Diving Experiences
Explore Everything We Have to Offer
Everything You Need to Become a Better Diver
Instead, sometimes just say:
Training
The Oriskany
Service
Local diving
Our shop
Short nouns feel confident.
________________


08 — Don't force the “X. X. X.” copy pattern
This is one I'd specifically add to your design rules.
Avoid overusing:
Train. Dive. Repeat.
Learn. Explore. Discover.
Gear. Service. Adventure.
It's trendy and can quickly become AI-ish.
Use it sparingly.
One memorable line = great.
Five of them = marketing template.
________________


09 — Stop making everything “premium”
Dive Pros isn't a luxury brand.
It's something arguably more interesting:
a deeply experienced local dive shop.
The brand should feel:
* knowledgeable
* adventurous
* welcoming
* technical
* local
* established
* slightly gritty
Not:
* luxury
* minimalist
* corporate
* startup
* “premium experience”
That distinction is important.
________________


10 — Let technical information stay technical
This is HUGE.
Don't dumb everything down in an attempt to make the website “clean.”
Dive Pros has genuinely nerdy expertise.
For example, the technical diving content includes things like:
* gas planning
* decompression
* trim
* buoyancy
* gas switching
* emergency procedures
* helium/nitrox
* rebreathers
That's credibility. (Florida Dive Pros)
Rule:
Simplify the presentation, not the expertise.
You can make technical information visually digestible without turning it into generic marketing fluff.
________________


11 — Testimonials should sound like humans
Don't “correct” customer reviews too much.
If someone says:
“These guys are the greatest.”
leave it.
If someone says:
“yalls help”
leave it if it's a direct quote.
The imperfections are actually valuable.
The original site has years of reviews with very different voices. That's social proof and personality. (Florida Dive Pros)
Rule:
Don't AI-polish human voices.
________________


12 — But edit everything else ruthlessly
There are two categories:
Customer voice
→ preserve it.
Dive Pros voice
→ edit it.
That distinction should be sacred.
________________


13 — Use contractions
Unless you're writing something technical/legal:
We are → We're
You are → You're
We have → We've
It is → It's
Do not → Don't
This makes the brand feel much more like a person talking.
________________


14 — Don't capitalize SCUBA randomly
Pick a style and stick to it.
I'd recommend:
SCUBA
rather than:
Scuba
scuba
SCUBA
The original site jumps between SCUBA, Scuba and SCUBA/Snorkeling. (Florida Dive Pros)
Same goes for:
* Dive Pros
* dive shop
* Gulf Coast
* Pensacola
* Open Water
* Advanced Open Water
Create a tiny style dictionary and enforce it everywhere.
________________


15 — Don't use apostrophes for Dive Pros
This is actually important because the old reviews contain:
“Dive Pro’s”
That's incorrect when referring to the plural business name.
Correct:
Dive Pros
Not:
Dive Pro's
But because it's inside a customer quote, don't alter it.
________________


16 — Write headlines in sentence case. Set them in all caps.
Copy is authored in sentence case:
Local dive sites
Open Water
The shop
The stylesheet sets those headlines in all caps:
LOCAL DIVE SITES
Don’t write the source as LOCAL DIVE SITES & EXPERIENCES.
Don’t switch display headlines to sentence-case styling to feel “less corporate.”
Body copy, quotes, names, and data tables stay sentence case.
________________


17 — Every section needs a job
Before adding a section ask:
What does this section help the visitor do?
Possible answers:
* Learn
* Compare
* Decide
* Book
* Contact
* Navigate
* Trust
* Explore
If the answer is:
“It makes the page look better.”
🚨 reconsider it.
________________


18 — Don't use decoration to fill empty space
This is probably the biggest visual rule I'd give you.
Don't add:
* random blobs
* stars
* gradients
* circles
* floating shapes
* random lines
unless they have a reason to exist.
Instead, use Dive Pros' actual world:
* dive tables
* depth measurements
* coordinates
* nautical charts
* tank markings
* equipment diagrams
* certification cards
* boat details
* wreck schematics
* underwater photography
* maps
* timestamps
* dive logs
That's your visual language.
And it's much harder for AI to fake convincingly.
________________


19 — Break the grid intentionally
Don't let every section become:
[text] [image]
[image] [text]
[text] [image]
[image] [text]
After a few sections, break it.
Maybe:
FULL-WIDTH ORISKANY IMAGE
911 FT
USS ORISKANY
Then a tiny technical specification row.
Then an asymmetrical image gallery.
Then text.
That's authored design.
________________


20 — Give important things different visual treatment
Not everything deserves to be a card.
Use:
Cards
For:
* courses
* services
* products
* categories
Full-bleed
For:
* Oriskany
* underwater photography
* major brand moments
Tables
For:
* pricing
* dive sites
* specifications
Big numbers
For:
* depths
* distances
* years
* tank capacity
Lists
For:
* equipment
* course requirements
* services
Maps
For:
* dive locations
This creates visual hierarchy naturally.
________________


21 — Don't make every CTA “Learn More”
This is a BIG one.
Instead of:
Learn More →
Use:
View courses →
See dive sites →
Explore the Oriskany →
View service pricing →
Meet the instructors →
Get directions →
Rule:
A CTA should tell me exactly what happens next.
________________


22 — Use “you” more than “we”
Old websites tend to say:
We offer...
We provide...
We have...
Try:
You can train in our indoor pool.
Bring your gear in for service.
Explore wrecks off Pensacola.
The visitor should feel like the protagonist.
________________


23 — But don't eliminate “we”
You still need personality.
Compare:
Dive Pros provides SCUBA instruction.
vs.
We've been teaching people to dive in Pensacola since 1988.
The second feels like a real business.
________________


24 — Give every page one sentence that only Dive Pros could say
This is my favorite rule.
Ask:
Could another dive shop copy this sentence?
If yes → make it more specific.
For example:
“Explore beautiful underwater environments.”
❌ Any dive shop.
“From the 911-foot Oriskany to shallow shore dives, Pensacola gives you wrecks, reefs, and plenty of reasons to get in the water.”
✅ Much harder to copy.
The local dive-site database is one of the site's strongest sources of this kind of specificity. (Florida Dive Pros)
________________


25 — Don't be afraid of “ugly” information
This is particularly important for your redesign.
A website doesn't have to hide:
* prices
* phone numbers
* hours
* addresses
* requirements
* restrictions
* equipment lists
* technical specs
The original site has valuable information like service pricing and course requirements. (Florida Dive Pros)
Make that information beautiful instead of deleting it.
________________


The ultimate rule
I'd put this at the top of your Figma file:
If it sounds impressive but doesn't tell me anything, rewrite it.
If it tells me something specific, make it bigger.
That single rule will eliminate a huge amount of the AI-generated feeling.
And there's an even more important one for the visual design:
Don't design a generic “dive shop.” Design Dive Pros.
The original site gives you an insane amount of raw material to do that: Pensacola, the Gulf Coast, the Oriskany, 1988, the training pool, local wrecks, technical diving, instructors, equipment service, maps, and decades of customer relationships. (Florida Dive Pros)
That's the stuff I'd lean into rather than trying to make the redesign look “modern.”
Modern is a visual style.
Specific is a brand.
